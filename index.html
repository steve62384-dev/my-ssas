What is a SSAS pension?

By Steve Hunt ACII TEP · Last checked 5 October 2026

The short answer: a SSAS (small self-administered scheme) is a pension scheme set up by an employer. The employer is usually a company owned and run by its directors. Directors and staff can be members, and the scheme's rules can let other people join too, such as family members. The members are usually the trustees, so they decide how the money is invested, within HMRC's rules. A SSAS can lend money to the company that set it up. It can also buy commercial property, such as the company's own premises.

Sources: Law: Finance Act 2004, s.150(5); s.179 · HMRC: PTM123200

What the letters mean

  • Small: SSASs are usually very small schemes. The government says there are around 60,000 SSAS savers across around 21,000 schemes. A scheme with fewer than 12 members can be let off having an auditor. To qualify, every member must be a trustee, or a director of the company that acts as trustee. The trustees must also decide everything together, or have an independent trustee.
  • Self-administered: the members run it themselves, as trustees. They can also pay a specialist SSAS firm to do the paperwork and deal with HMRC.
  • Scheme: it is an occupational pension scheme. That means an employer sets it up to provide pensions, although a specialist firm can help run it.

Who can have one?

You need an employer to set one up. That is usually a limited company you own and run.

The members are usually the directors, and staff can join too. Family members can often join, if the scheme's rules allow it.

The scheme must be registered with HMRC to get the pension tax reliefs.

What can a SSAS do?

  • Invest in shares, funds and cash.
  • Buy commercial property, such as offices, shops, factories or land, including your company's own premises. Your company then pays the pension a market rent. SSAS and commercial property
  • Lend money to your company. This is called a loanback. Only an occupational scheme, such as a SSAS, can lend to its employer within HMRC's rules. If a SIPP lent money to a company you control, HMRC would treat the loan as an unauthorised payment. SSAS loanback rules
  • Borrow up to half of its net value, for example to help buy a property.

A SSAS cannot sensibly own a home, a holiday let, or things like cars, art or wine. HMRC calls these "taxable property", and the tax charges are heavy.

What does a SSAS cost?

We checked the current fees of ten providers. For a SSAS with two members, the yearly fee is between £800 and £2,420, plus VAT. Most also charge initial costs. Of those we can price, the highest in our simple example is £2,100 plus VAT.

The SSAS fees on those sheets are flat fees, not a percentage of the pension. Compare that with a 1% yearly charge. A flat fee of £1,500 a year costs less once the pension is above £150,000, and more below it.

The downsides

  • The fees are flat, so a small pension pays more, as a share of its value.
  • You need an employer, usually your own company, behind it.
  • A loan to your own company can go wrong. If the company fails, the pension can lose that money.
  • As a trustee, you must keep the scheme within the rules. Break HMRC's rules and the tax charges are heavy.
