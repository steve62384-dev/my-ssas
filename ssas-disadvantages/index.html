What are the disadvantages of a SSAS?

By Steve Hunt ACII TEP · Last checked 5 October 2026

The short answer: a SSAS gives you control, but it comes with cost, work and risk. The fees are flat, so they weigh more on a small pension. You are usually a trustee, so you carry legal duties and can be personally liable. Breaking the tax rules is costly. A SSAS that lends to your company or owns its premises ties your pension to your business. And the scheme itself is not protected by the FSCS.

Sources Provider evidence: SSAS provider fees compared, 2026 · TPR: Trustee toolkit, tutorial 5: trustee liabilities and protections · HMRC: PTM131000: unauthorised payments; PTM123200: loans to sponsoring employers · FSCS: Pensions

Cost

Flat fees

We checked the current fees of ten providers. For a SSAS with two members, the yearly fee is £800 to £2,420 plus VAT. Most also charge initial costs, and extra for buying property or making a loan. The fees are flat, so the smaller the pension, the bigger the fee is as a share of it.

Sources Provider evidence: SSAS provider fees compared, 2026

Other costs

Buying property also brings legal and valuation fees, and stamp duty land tax. Before most investments, the trustees must get and consider proper advice, given or confirmed in writing. That advice can cost money too.

Sources GOV.UK: Stamp Duty Land Tax: non-residential and mixed rates · Law: Pensions Act 1995, s.36(3)

Responsibility

You carry the legal duties

In a SSAS you are usually a trustee. A trustee can have to pay personally for a loss caused by their own breach of trust. More than one trustee can be liable for the same loss. The trust deed cannot remove liability for failing to take proper care with investments. Some fines and penalties on a trustee cannot be paid from the scheme's money.

Sources TPR: Trustee toolkit, tutorial 5: trustee liabilities and protections · Law: Pensions Act 1995, s.33; Pensions Act 2004, s.256

Breaking the tax rules is costly

An unauthorised payment normally causes a 40% tax charge on the member or employer. A further 15% surcharge can apply if enough unauthorised payments are made. The scheme administrator can also face a scheme sanction charge: normally 15% where the 40% charge has been paid, but up to 40% otherwise. In serious cases HMRC can de-register the scheme, if one of the grounds in the law applies. One example is when the scheme's chargeable payments pass a set limit. The scheme administrator then faces a charge of 40% of the cash and the market value of the assets held just before.

Sources Law: Finance Act 2004, ss.208, 209, 239 and 240; Finance Act 2004, ss.158 and 242 · HMRC: PTM131000: unauthorised payments; PTM135100: scheme sanction charge; PTM033200: de-registration; PTM033300: consequences of de-registration

Paperwork and deadlines

Someone must keep clear records, for the tax year they relate to and six more years. The scheme must report to HMRC when certain events happen, and send returns when asked. A money purchase SSAS with 2 to 11 members normally completes a scheme return for The Pensions Regulator every three years. Scheme money must be kept in its own bank account, apart from the employer's money. Since 6 April 2026, the scheme administrator must be UK resident.

Sources HMRC: PTM160200: keeping records; Send pension scheme reports; Register as a pension scheme administrator · TPR: DC scheme return · Law: Pensions Act 1995, s.49(1); Finance Act 2004, s.270

Trustees have to agree

To lend to your company, or to be let off having an auditor, a SSAS must meet the small scheme conditions. One way is for every decision to be unanimous among the member trustees. Where a company is the only trustee, every decision must be unanimous among the directors who are members. So one member trustee, or one member director, can block a decision. The other way is to have the required independent trustee or director.

Sources Law: SI 2005/3378, regs 1(2) and 12; SI 1996/1715, reg 3(1)(h)

Risk

Your pension and your business are tied together

A SSAS can lend your company up to 50% of the cash and market value of its assets. It can also own your company's premises. If the company struggles, the pension can suffer too. A loan may not be repaid on time, and rolling it over more than once brings tax charges. Rent left unpaid, and not collected on normal terms, can be treated as a loan to the company. Pension law expects the trustees to think about spreading the risk.

Sources Law: Finance Act 2004, s.179; SI 2005/3378, regs 6 and 7 · HMRC: PTM123200: loans to sponsoring employers; PTM121000: investments, essential principles; PTM123100: loans, general principles

Property can be slow to sell

The Pensions Regulator says most private market assets, such as property, cannot easily or quickly be turned into cash. A quick sale may mean a lower price. So the trustees must plan for the cash the scheme will need, for example to pay benefits or transfers out.

Sources TPR: Private markets investment; Investment governance in DC schemes

No FSCS cover for the scheme

The scheme itself is not protected by the FSCS if it fails. Cash a SSAS holds in a UK bank can be protected. For a money purchase SSAS, the FSCS can treat each eligible member separately, up to £120,000 per member per banking licence. A member's own savings at the same bank count towards the same limit.

Sources FSCS: Pensions; Deposit limit · PRA: PRA Rulebook: Depositor Protection, rule 6.4

If the SSAS firm fails

The scheme's money and assets are held on trust. They are kept apart from the SSAS firm's own money, even if the firm is also a trustee. But moving to a new firm can be slow and costly. Hartley Pensions went into administration on 29 July 2022. In July 2026, four years on, its administrators were still working on a sale or transfer of its SSAS business.

Sources FCA: Hartley Pensions Limited enters administration · Administrators: Hartley Pensions: progress report, 29 January to 28 July 2026

Scammers target SSASs

MoneyHelper warns that, because SSASs are not regulated by the FCA, scammers often use them to reach scam investments. Being asked to join the SSAS of a company you have no real link to is a warning sign. Inventing a job link to join is fraud. In June 2026 the government said up to 1 in 10 transfers into SSASs could be fraud.

Sources MoneyHelper: SSAS pensions explained · DWP: Protecting pension savers: options assessment, 9 June 2026

Getting started

HMRC refuses some new schemes

HMRC must register a new scheme unless certain problems apply. Examples are wrong or false information, or a scheme administrator who is not fit and proper. HMRC received 2,421 applications to register new pension schemes in 2025/26. It registered 54% and refused 33%. Those figures cover all kinds of scheme, not just SSASs.

Sources Law: Finance Act 2004, s.153(5) · HMRC: PTM032200: HMRC's decision; Pension schemes newsletter 180, April 2026

Transfers in face extra checks

Before your old scheme lets money move into a SSAS, it must run the scam checks in the 2021 transfer rules. These include proof that you have a real link to the SSAS's employer. In June 2026 the government asked for views on a new red flag, to stop a transfer where there is no real job link. By 5 October 2026 it had not published its response.

Sources Law: SI 2021/1237 · TPR: Dealing with transfer requests · DWP: Protecting pension savers: consultation, 9 June 2026

When things change

If your company is sold or closes

A SSAS does not end just because the company is sold or closes, unless its rules say so. But the trustees may need a new sponsoring employer, or to run it as a closed scheme, transfer it or wind it up. Any loan to the company must keep to HMRC's loan rules until it is paid back. HMRC can take a SSAS off its register if a sponsoring employer is a dormant company.

Sources HMRC: PTM022000: types of pension scheme; PTM123200: loans to sponsoring employers; PTM033200: de-registration · TPR: Winding up a defined contribution scheme: deciding whether to wind up

Common questions

Is a SSAS worth it for a small pension?

No law sets a minimum size for a SSAS. But the fees are flat, so they weigh more on a small pension. A flat fee of £1,500 a year costs less than a 1% yearly charge once the pension is above £150,000, and more below it. Some providers set their own minimum.

Sources Provider evidence: SSAS provider fees compared, 2026 · Steve's analysis: The flat fee and 1% comparison

Can I lose money in a SSAS?

Yes. The investments a SSAS holds can fall in value, so you could get back less than was paid in. The scheme itself is not protected by the FSCS.

Sources MoneyHelper: SSAS pensions explained · FSCS: Pensions

Can I move out of a SSAS later?

Usually, yes. Members can normally move their share to another registered pension scheme, such as a SIPP. Assets such as business property can move across as they are, if the new scheme accepts them, or be sold first. A loan from the SSAS to your company cannot move into a SIPP. This site does not advise on transfers.

Sources HMRC: PTM100010: transfers; PTM123200: loans to sponsoring employers · Law: Finance Act 2004, s.161(5)

Steve's analysis

  • The flat fee and 1% comparison: our own arithmetic. £1,500 is 1% of £150,000, so a £1,500 flat fee is cheaper than a 1% charge above that size and dearer below it. It is an illustration, not a recommendation.
